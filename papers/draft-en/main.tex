\title{A Physically Faithful Device Simulator for a\\
Third-Party GNSS-Disciplined Rubidium Oscillator:\\
Construction and Fidelity Validation from Limited Data}

% Yazarlar ELECO surumunde doldurulacak.
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{[Yazar]}
\IEEEauthorblockA{[Kurum]\\ [E-posta]}}

\maketitle

%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{abstract}
Software that controls or monitors precision timing instruments is
difficult to test, because the physical device is expensive, singular,
and not always available. We present a physically faithful software
\emph{simulator} of a commercial GNSS-disciplined rubidium oscillator
(a Jackson Labs Low Noise Rubidium GNSDO) that answers the instrument's
serial/SCPI interface indistinguishably from the real unit, so that
client software can be developed and tested without hardware. In
software-testing terms the simulator is a \emph{fake}: a working but
simplified implementation used as a test double. Unlike a digital twin,
it has no automated data link to any physical instance; by the standard
taxonomy it is a \emph{digital model}. Our contribution is not the
underlying clock model, which is textbook, but a repeatable method for
building such a model of a \emph{third-party} device from only (i) the
vendor manual and (ii) a small set of protocol recordings, and for
validating its fidelity when access to the device is limited. Every
model parameter is traced to a documented source; parameters with no
source are marked as assumptions. We show that the resulting model
reproduces device behaviours that were \emph{not} targeted during
construction---most strikingly the manual's own worked health-status
example---and we document systematic manual-versus-device discrepancies
that a manual-only simulator would get wrong.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
device simulator, test double, digital model, GNSS-disciplined

\section{Introduction}
Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) disciplined oscillators
(GNSSDOs) provide traceable time and frequency to applications ranging
from telecommunications to instrumentation. A GNSSDO combines the
long-term accuracy of GNSS with the short-term stability of a local
oscillator---here a rubidium (Rb) atomic reference. Any software that
integrates such a device (monitoring dashboards, automated test
equipment, logging and alarm systems) must be developed and tested
against the device's command interface. This creates a practical
problem: the physical instrument is costly, there is often only one, it
must warm up and lock before it is usable, and it cannot be
monopolised by a software team for days of testing.

The natural answer is to replace the device, during development, with a
software stand-in that speaks the same interface. The engineering
question is \emph{how faithful} that stand-in must be, and---because the
device is a third-party product whose firmware we cannot see and whose
unit we cannot always access---\emph{how we can know} that it is
faithful at all.

This paper reports the construction and validation of such a stand-in
for a commercial Low Noise Rubidium GNSDO. Concretely, our
contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A layered, extensible simulator that answers the device's
serial/SCPI interface and reproduces its time-dependent physical
behaviour (holdover error accumulation, measurement noise, warm-up),
runnable over a virtual serial port so that unmodified client software
connects to it as if to the real device.
\item A repeatable \emph{construction method} for a third-party device:
every observable value is derived either from the vendor manual or from
a small set of protocol recordings, and any value lacking a source is
explicitly labelled an assumption.
\item A \emph{fidelity-validation method} suited to limited device
access, based on reproducing behaviours that were not targeted during
construction, and on cross-checking the manual against real recordings.
\end{itemize}

We are deliberately precise about what the artefact is
(Section~\ref{sec:related}): it is a \emph{digital model} and, in
software-testing terms, a \emph{fake}; it is \emph{not} a digital twin.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
\subsection{The device}
The reference device is a Jackson Labs Low Noise Rubidium
GNSDO~\cite{jacksonlabs2015lnrb}. Its serial interface follows the SCPI
convention (ASCII commands terminated by \texttt{CR}/\texttt{LF},
case-insensitive, short and long forms). Two one-pulse-per-second (1PPS)
signals coexist internally: one derived by the GNSS receiver (accurate
in the long term, noisy second-to-second) and one produced by the Rb
oscillator (quiet in the short term, drifting in the long term). A
control loop steers the Rb oscillator to minimise the time interval
between them.

\subsection{TINT and holdover}
The queryable quantity \texttt{SYNC:TINT?} reports this time interval
(TINT), the device's fundamental error signal. While the device is
locked, the closed loop drives TINT toward zero. When GNSS is lost, the
loop opens: no correction is applied and the oscillator's small
frequency error integrates into a growing \emph{time} error. This
coasting regime is called \emph{holdover}, and modelling its error
growth is the first behaviour we target.

\section{Related Work and Positioning}
\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Digital twins, shadows, and models}
The term ``digital twin'' is widely misused. We adopt the taxonomy of
Kritzinger~\emph{et~al.}~\cite{kritzinger2018dt} and the formalization
of Barbie and Hasselbring~\cite{barbie2024dtp}, which distinguish, by
the degree of automated data exchange with a specific physical
instance: a \emph{digital model} (no automated link), a \emph{digital
shadow} (automated one-way physical$\rightarrow$digital), and a
\emph{digital twin} (automated bidirectional). Our simulator has no
automated link to any physical unit---recordings were taken manually,
once---so it is a \emph{digital model}.

Barbie and Hasselbring further define a \emph{Digital Twin Prototype}
(DTP): a software prototype whose emulated sensors are driven by
existing recordings, usable for continuous-integration testing without
the physical object~\cite{barbie2024dtp,barbie2023smartfarming}. Our
purpose and architecture align closely with the DTP idea---including
keeping the RS-232 protocol at the host layer so device drivers are
unchanged. The crucial difference is access: a DTP assumes the device's
\emph{own} firmware (``the configurations are equal''), whereas we
reconstruct behaviour black-box, from outside the protocol boundary, for
a device we did not build. Khedr~\emph{et~al.}~\cite{khedr2026sos}
observe that digital-twin engineering still lacks practical guidance on
formalising and verifying qualities such as \emph{substitutability} and
\emph{fidelity}; these are exactly the qualities our validation method
targets.

\subsection{Test doubles}
In software-testing terminology~\cite{meszaros2007xunit,fowler2007mocks}
our artefact is a \emph{fake}: a working but simplified implementation,
as opposed to a stub or mock that returns canned answers. Its earlier,
constant-response version behaved like a stub; the work reported here
turned it into a fake. Deriving a test double's behaviour from
recordings of the real system parallels RICK~\cite{tiwari2023rick},
which generates mocks from production traces; we do so by hand from
device protocol recordings.

\subsection{Oscillator and holdover modelling}
Holdover and oscillator stability modelling is a mature field, with
Kalman-filter, machine-learning, and quantization-noise refinements.
We claim \emph{no} contribution there: we apply the textbook two-state
clock model with parameters taken from the vendor manual. Our
contribution is the construction-and-validation method around it.

\section{Simulator Architecture}
The simulator is layered so that device physics is isolated from
protocol handling:
\begin{itemize}
\item a serial server that owns a pseudo-terminal (created with
\texttt{socat}) and moves bytes to/from lines;
\item an SCPI parser that dispatches a command to a registered handler
and returns \texttt{Command Error} for unknown commands, as the real
device does;
\item a device-state object holding the instrument's memory;
\item a \texttt{models} package of pure functions encoding the physics,
with no I/O and time passed as an argument.
\end{itemize}
The last point matters for validation: because the physics functions are
pure and take elapsed time as a parameter, ``what would the device read
after five hours?'' is answered in a test without waiting in real time.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Physical Models}
\label{sec:models}
All numeric parameters are traced to the manual or to recordings; the
only sourceless parameter (ambient start temperature) is flagged as an
assumption.

\subsection{Holdover error accumulation}
Phase is the integral of frequency. With relative frequency error
$y_0$ at the moment of GNSS loss and oscillator ageing $D$, the
accumulated time error is
\begin{equation}
x(t) = x_0 + y_0\,t + \tfrac{1}{2} D\,t^2 ,
\label{eq:holdover}
\end{equation}
where $x_0$ is the phase frozen at entry. Here $y_0$ is the device's
Frequency Error Estimate (\texttt{SYNC:FEE?}) and $D$ is the
$8\times10^{-14}$/day ageing figure from the manual. The quadratic term
is negligible over hours and becomes visible only over days
(Fig.~\ref{fig:holdover}).

\subsection{Deterministic measurement noise}
Sensor readings must not be constant, but noise is modelled as a
function of time, not as a fresh random draw per query:
$v(t) = \text{base} + \text{amp}\cdot f(t)$, where $f$ is a sum of
sinusoids with golden-ratio-irrational frequency ratios. This gives two
properties a random stream cannot: two queries at the same instant
return the same value (physically necessary), and a run is exactly
repeatable (debuggable). Amplitudes come from observed ranges;
time-scales are separated physically (temperature wanders slowly,
voltage quickly).

\subsection{Warm-up and servo states}
From a cold start, temperature approaches its steady value
exponentially, $T(t)=T_\infty-(T_\infty-T_0)e^{-t/\tau}$, because heat
loss scales with the temperature difference. The thermal rise is fast
(a few minutes, consistent with the manual's ``$<2$~min to atomic
lock''); GNSS \emph{lock} is a separate, slower process ($\sim$20~min).
The device passes through servo states (\texttt{SERV:STATE?})
warm-up~$\rightarrow$~locking~$\rightarrow$~locked, which
\texttt{SYNC:LOCKED?} alone cannot distinguish
(Fig.~\ref{fig:warmup}).

%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Fidelity Validation}
\label{sec:validation}
With limited device access, exhaustive comparison against the real unit
is impossible. We use three complementary checks.

\subsection{Traceability}
Every parameter is sourced (manual clause or recording); sourceless
values are labelled assumptions and made configurable, so a wrong
assumption is a one-line fix rather than a hidden constant.

\subsection{Unintended-prediction reproduction}
The strongest evidence that a model is right is that it reproduces an
observation it was not tuned for. Three arose:
(i) after a multi-hour holdover the simulator returns health status
\texttt{0x14}, exactly the worked example in the manual
(\texttt{0x10}\,$|$\,\texttt{0x4}); (ii) the per-second growth rate of
TINT equals the device's declared FEE; (iii) CSAC temperature tracks
PCB temperature at a fixed $+1.32\,^\circ$C offset seen across all
recorded pairs. None were targeted.

\subsection{Manual-versus-device discrepancies}
Building from recordings revealed places where the manual alone is
insufficient: TINT is signed and swings about zero (the manual's
``0.2\,ns average'' is a mean, not an amplitude);
\texttt{MEAS:CURR?} returns a temperature, not a current;
\texttt{EFControl} absolute equals relative $\times 200$; the two
satellite counters can disagree. Each is evidence that a faithful
simulator must be aligned to real behaviour, not documentation alone.

\section{Limitations}
The artefact is a digital model, not a shadow or twin: there is no live
link to a physical unit. The measurement noise is deterministic and
does \emph{not} reproduce the device's spectral (Allan-deviation)
signature; this is intentional, since the purpose is client-software
testing, for which repeatability outweighs spectral realism. Some
recorded values were untimestamped, so we validate ranges and
relationships rather than time-anchored points.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
We built a physically faithful, behaviourally validated simulator of a
third-party GNSS-disciplined rubidium oscillator from only its manual
and a handful of protocol recordings, and gave a method for validating
fidelity under limited access. A natural next step toward a digital
\emph{shadow} is to let the simulator ingest periodic device logs and
self-calibrate, closing the one-way data path that a digital model, by
definition, lacks.
