Third-Party GNSS-Disciplined Rubidium Oscillator:\\
Construction and Fidelity Validation from Limited Data}

\begin{abstract}
Testing software that drives a precision timing instrument is awkward in
practice: the device is costly, often singular, slow to reach a usable
state, and cannot be monopolised for the days of fault-injecting runs
that thorough testing demands. The obvious remedy---a software stand-in
that speaks the same interface---raises a question the literature rarely
confronts: when the device is a third-party product whose firmware is
inaccessible and whose single unit is only intermittently available, how
can the stand-in's fidelity be established at all? We answer this for a
commercial Low Noise Rubidium GNSS-disciplined oscillator. In the
software-testing sense the artefact is a \emph{fake}, and in the
digital-twin sense a \emph{digital model}; it is not a digital
twin---having no automated link to any physical unit---and we are
deliberate about the distinction. Its contribution is not the clock
model, which is textbook, but a repeatable method for building such a
model from only a vendor manual and a handful of protocol recordings,
and for arguing its fidelity without the exhaustive hardware comparison
the literature assumes: trace every parameter to a source, mark the rest
as assumptions, compare directly against recorded outputs, test physical
invariants automatically, seek behaviours the model reproduces yet was
never tuned for, and document where the manual and the device disagree.
Without ever being fitted to it, the model reproduces the manual's own
worked health-status example.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
device simulator, test double, digital model, GNSS-disciplined
oscillator, holdover, SCPI, software testing, reproducibility.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) disciplined oscillators
(GNSSDOs) deliver traceable time and frequency to telecommunications,
power, instrumentation, and defence systems. A GNSSDO combines the
long-term accuracy of GNSS with the short-term stability of a local
oscillator; in the device studied here that oscillator is a rubidium
(Rb) atomic reference, steered by a software control loop to a
GNSS-derived reference. Any application built on such a device---a
monitoring dashboard, an automated test system, a logging or alarming
service---must be implemented and tested against the device's serial
command interface.

This dependency is awkward in practice. The instrument is costly and
often singular; it must warm up and then discipline its oscillator
before it reports usable values, a process of tens of minutes; and it
cannot be monopolised by a software team for the days of repeated,
fault-injecting runs that thorough testing requires. Worse, several of
the behaviours a client most needs to handle---loss of GNSS, holdover,
health degradation---are precisely the ones that are hardest to trigger
on demand against real hardware.

The established response is to substitute, during development, a
software stand-in that speaks the same interface, so that unmodified
client code cannot tell the difference. This turns the problem into two
engineering questions. First, \emph{how faithful} must the stand-in be:
which behaviours must it reproduce, and to what precision, for it to be
useful for its intended purpose? Second, and more delicate when the
device is a third-party product whose firmware cannot be inspected and
whose single unit is only intermittently available, \emph{how can that
fidelity be established} without the exhaustive hardware comparison that
the literature usually assumes?

We use ``physically faithful'' in a deliberately purpose-relative
sense. The simulator reproduces the device's time-domain behaviour and
its documented relationships to the precision a client requires, but it
does \emph{not} reproduce the device's spectral (Allan-deviation)
signature. This is a conscious design choice rather than a shortcoming:
the artefact exists to test client software, for which reproducibility
and determinism are worth more than spectral realism, and for which a
random-per-query noise source would in fact be a defect
(Section~\ref{sec:models}). We state this here because it shapes every
subsequent modelling decision.

This paper answers both questions for a commercial Jackson Labs Low
Noise Rubidium GNSDO. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A layered, physically grounded simulator}
(Sections~\ref{sec:arch}--\ref{sec:models}) that answers the device's
serial/SCPI interface and reproduces its time-dependent
behaviour---holdover error accumulation, deterministic measurement
noise, and warm-up---and that runs over a virtual serial port so that
unmodified client software connects to it as to the real device.
\item \textbf{A construction method for a third-party device}
(Section~\ref{sec:models}): every observable value is derived either
from the vendor manual or from a small set of protocol recordings, and
every value that has no such source is explicitly labelled an
assumption and exposed as configuration.
\item \textbf{A fidelity-validation method for limited access}
(Section~\ref{sec:validation}). The setting is deliberately
constrained---a third-party device, no firmware, intermittent access,
and largely untimestamped recordings---so the exhaustive time-aligned
hardware comparison assumed by most of the literature is impossible.
In its place we combine a direct comparison against the recorded
outputs we do have, automated physical-invariant tests, the
reproduction of behaviours that were not tuned for, and a catalogue of
manual-versus-device discrepancies that a documentation-only simulator
would get wrong.
\end{itemize}

We are deliberately precise about what the artefact is. By the
digital-twin taxonomy it is a \emph{digital model}, and in
software-testing terms a \emph{fake}; it is \emph{not} a digital twin.
Section~\ref{sec:related} justifies this terminology, which we regard as
a matter of scientific accuracy rather than modesty.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
\subsection{Device and interface}
The reference device is a Jackson Labs Low Noise Rubidium
GNSDO~\cite{jacksonlabs2015lnrb}. It exposes a serial interface at
$115{,}200$~baud, 8N1, using the SCPI convention: ASCII commands
terminated by \texttt{CR}, \texttt{LF}, or \texttt{CR}/\texttt{LF},
case-insensitive, with both abbreviated and long spellings
(e.g.\ \texttt{SYNC:TINT?} and \texttt{SYNChronization:TINTerval?}).
Queries end in \texttt{?}; a smaller set of commands change device
state.

\subsection{Disciplining and the time-interval error}
Two one-pulse-per-second (1PPS) signals coexist inside the unit. One is
produced by the GNSS receiver: accurate over the long term because it
is tied to GNSS system time, but noisy from second to second. The other
is produced by the Rb oscillator: extremely quiet over the short term,
but subject to slow drift. A control loop steers the Rb oscillator so
that its 1PPS tracks the GNSS 1PPS. The instantaneous difference between
the two, the \emph{time-interval error} (TINT, queryable as
\texttt{SYNC:TINT?}), is the loop's error signal and is reported with a
resolution of $10^{-10}$~s.

\subsection{Holdover}
While the device is locked, the closed loop drives TINT towards zero. If
GNSS is lost the loop opens: no correction is applied, and the
oscillator's residual frequency error integrates into a growing
\emph{time} error. This coasting regime is called \emph{holdover}. Its
error growth---and the resulting degradation of the device's reported
health---is the first and most safety-relevant behaviour a client must
handle, and the first we model.

\subsection{Health status and warm-up}
The device summarises its condition in a hexadecimal bit-mask
(\texttt{SYNC:HEALTH?}), each bit encoding one fault or transient
condition (for example, phase offset beyond $210$~ns, or holdover
longer than $60$~s). From a cold start the unit is not immediately
usable: the atomic reference reaches operating temperature in under two
minutes, but full GNSS lock takes on the order of twenty minutes, and
the device advances through distinct servo states in between. These
timescales matter to client software and are modelled in
Section~\ref{sec:models}.

\section{Related Work and Positioning}
\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Digital models, shadows, and twins}
The term ``digital twin'' is applied so broadly that it has become
ambiguous; broad syntheses appear in~\cite{tao2019dt,rasheed2020dt}. We adopt the categorical distinction of
Kritzinger~\emph{et~al.}~\cite{kritzinger2018dt}, formalised by Barbie
and Hasselbring~\cite{barbie2024dtp}, which classifies a virtual
counterpart by the degree of \emph{automated} data exchange with a
specific physical instance: a \emph{digital model} has no automated
link; a \emph{digital shadow} has an automated one-way
physical-to-digital link; a \emph{digital twin} has an automated,
bidirectional link over the instance's life cycle. Our artefact has no
automated link of any kind---its parameters were extracted, once and by
hand, from a manual and a handful of recordings---so it is, precisely, a
digital model. The interactive command/response exchange between a test
client and the simulator is unrelated to this classification, which
concerns the link between a physical instance and its digital
counterpart.

Within the same body of work, Barbie and Hasselbring define a
\emph{Digital Twin Prototype} (DTP): a software prototype whose emulated
sensors are driven by prior recordings, used for continuous-integration
testing without the physical
object~\cite{barbie2024dtp,barbie2023smartfarming}. Our purpose and
architecture are close to the DTP idea, down to keeping the serial
protocol at the host layer so that device drivers are unchanged. The
decisive difference is one of access and provenance: a DTP presumes the
device's \emph{own} firmware (``the configurations are equal''), whereas
we reconstruct behaviour from outside the protocol boundary, black-box,
for a device we did not design and cannot open. Khedr~\emph{et~al.}
observe that digital-twin engineering still lacks practical guidance for
formalising and verifying qualities such as \emph{substitutability} and
\emph{fidelity}~\cite{khedr2026sos}; these are exactly the qualities our
validation method addresses, in the specific and common setting of a
third-party instrument.

\subsection{Test doubles}
In the vocabulary of software testing~\cite{meszaros2007xunit,%
fowler2007mocks} the artefact is a \emph{fake}: a working but simplified
implementation of a depended-on component, as distinct from a
\emph{stub} or \emph{mock}, which return pre-arranged answers without
modelling behaviour. This distinction also describes the project's own
history: an earlier version returned constant values and was, in effect,
a stub; the work reported here made it a fake. Deriving a double's
behaviour from recordings of the real system is the principle behind
RICK~\cite{tiwari2023rick}, which generates mocks from production
traces; we apply the same principle manually, to device protocol
recordings.

\subsection{Oscillator and holdover modelling}
Modelling oscillator stability and holdover is a mature area. We use the
standard two-state clock model, in which the oscillator is described by
phase and frequency states driven by characteristic noise
processes~\cite{zucca2005clock}, with parameters taken from the vendor
manual; refinements using Kalman filtering, machine learning, or
explicit quantisation-noise models are not needed for our purpose, and
we claim \emph{no} contribution there. We further distinguish our work from GNSS
\emph{signal} simulators, which synthesise radio-frequency satellite
signals; our artefact is a device- and protocol-level simulator that
synthesises SCPI responses. The contribution lies in the method by which
the model is built and validated, not in the model itself.

\section{Simulator Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}
The simulator is organised in layers so that device physics is isolated
from protocol handling:
\begin{itemize}
\item a \emph{serial server} that owns a pseudo-terminal, created with
\texttt{socat}, and converts between the byte stream and lines; a
client opens this virtual port exactly as it would a real
\texttt{/dev/tty} device;
\item an \emph{SCPI parser} that normalises a command and dispatches it
to a registered handler, resolving abbreviated and long forms, and
returning \texttt{Command Error} for unrecognised input, as the real
device does;
\item a \emph{device-state} object that holds the instrument's memory
and is the single point from which all responses are derived, so that a
summary query and an individual query can never disagree;
\item a \texttt{models} package of \emph{pure functions} encoding the
physics, performing no input/output and receiving elapsed time as an
argument.
\end{itemize}
The purity of the model layer is a deliberate design choice with a
direct bearing on validation (Section~\ref{sec:validation}): because a
model function is a function of elapsed time alone, the question ``what
would the device read after five hours of holdover?'' is answered in a
unit test in microseconds, without waiting and without a running server.
New command groups are added by registering handlers, so the protocol
surface grows without changes to the core.

\section{Physical Models}
\label{sec:models}
Every numeric parameter below is traced to a source in
Table~\ref{tab:params}; the single parameter with no documentary source,
the ambient start temperature, is marked as an assumption and is
configurable.

\subsection{Holdover error accumulation}
Phase is the integral of frequency. Let $y_0$ be the fractional
frequency error at the instant GNSS is lost and let $D$ be the
oscillator's ageing rate. The accumulated time error is
\begin{equation}
x(t) = x_0 + y_0\,t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,D\,t^{2},
\label{eq:holdover}
\end{equation}
where $x_0$ is the phase frozen at the moment of loss, since phase is
continuous and does not jump to zero. The linear term dominates over
minutes-to-hours; the quadratic ageing term is negligible there and
becomes visible only over days. We take $y_0$ to be the device's own
Frequency Error Estimate (\texttt{SYNC:FEE?})---the manual defines it as
an Allan-variance-like figure over a $1000$~s interval---and $D$ to be
the manual's $8\times10^{-14}$/day ageing specification. Because $y_0$
is read from the device, the accumulation rate is consistent with what
the device reports, and a client can in principle predict its own
holdover budget from a single FEE query.

\subsection{Deterministic measurement noise}
A real instrument's temperature, voltage, and TINT readings are never
perfectly constant, but modelling their variation as an independent
random draw per query would be wrong on two counts. Physically, the
device has a definite value at a given instant: two queries at the same
time must return the same number, whereas an independent draw would make
the value depend on how often it is polled. Methodologically, an
independent draw is not reproducible, which frustrates debugging. We
therefore model each reading as a deterministic function of elapsed
time,
\begin{equation}
v(t) = v_{\mathrm{base}} + A \sum_{k} w_k
       \sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi\,\phi^{k}\,t}{P} + \varphi_k\right),
\label{eq:noise}
\end{equation}
where the frequency ratios are powers of the golden ratio $\phi$, so
that the composite pattern is quasi-periodic and does not visibly
repeat. Three terms are used ($k=0,1,2$) with fixed weights
$w_k=1,\tfrac12,\tfrac14$; the per-channel phase offsets $\varphi_k$
are derived deterministically from the channel name, and the complete
constants are given with the released source. This yields both required
properties: identical values for identical instants, and exact
run-to-run repeatability. Amplitudes $A$ are taken from observed ranges
and the periods $P$ are separated by physical timescale---slow for
temperature, which has thermal inertia, faster for electrical
quantities.

\subsection{Warm-up and servo states}
\label{sec:warmup}
From a cold start the temperature approaches its steady value
exponentially,
\begin{equation}
T(t) = T_\infty - (T_\infty - T_0)\,e^{-t/\tau},
\label{eq:warmup}
\end{equation}
because heat loss scales with the temperature difference; a linear ramp
would be unphysical, overshooting rather than asymptoting. With
$\tau\approx 90$~s the modelled board temperature rises quickly, on the
order of a few minutes. The manual's ``less than two minutes to atomic
lock'' does not contradict this even though \eqref{eq:warmup} has closed
only about $73\%$ of its range at $t=2$~min: atomic lock is governed by
the internally regulated temperature of the physics package, whose
dedicated oven settles faster than the board-level PCB temperature we
model, so ``atomic lock'' can be reached while the PCB is still
approaching equilibrium. GNSS \emph{lock} is a separate and slower
process of roughly twenty minutes. The device therefore advances through
servo states (\texttt{SERV:STATE?}) from warm-up, to locking, to locked
(Fig.~\ref{fig:warmup}); \texttt{SYNC:LOCKED?} alone reports only the
last of these transitions and cannot distinguish ``still warming'' from
``warm but not yet locked''.

\section{Fidelity Validation}
\label{sec:validation}
The central methodological question of this work is how to argue that a
simulator is faithful when the device it imitates is a third-party
product available only intermittently, so that the exhaustive
side-by-side comparison assumed by much of the literature is
impossible. We use five complementary strategies.

\subsection{Direct comparison with recorded outputs}
Where the recordings permit it, we compare the simulator's response to a
command directly against the device's recorded response to the same
command (Table~\ref{tab:fidelity}). For identity and configuration
fields the match is exact, byte for byte; for structured multi-line
replies the field layout and formatting match, which is what a client
parser actually depends on; for derived quantities the documented
relationships are reproduced (the electronic-frequency-control absolute
value equals the relative value scaled by $200$ on all four recorded
pairs, and the CSAC temperature exceeds the PCB temperature by
$1.32\,^\circ$C to within $0.07\,^\circ$C); and for stochastic
quantities the simulator reproduces the observed range and sign rather
than a single value. This turns fidelity from an assertion into a
measured, if partial, comparison.

\subsection{Traceability of parameters}
Every parameter is tied to a documentary source---a manual clause or a
protocol recording (Table~\ref{tab:params}). The one parameter without
such a source is labelled an assumption and exposed as configuration, so
that a disputed value is a single, visible edit rather than a constant
buried in code. This makes the model's empirical commitments auditable.

\subsection{Physical-invariant testing}
Each behaviour is exercised by an automated measurement that asserts the
physical invariants it must satisfy---holdover monotonic and signed
correctly, locked TINT zero-mean and trend-free, temperature rising and
bounded, repeated runs identical---and that writes its data to a
versioned artefact stamped with the source-control revision. Because the
model functions are pure, these measurements sweep hours or days of
simulated time instantly. The numerical results below are regenerated
from those artefacts by a single command, so every reported figure is
reproducible from the recorded data.

\subsection{Reproduction of untuned behaviour}
The strongest available evidence that a model is correct, short of full
hardware comparison, is that it reproduces an observation for which it
was never tuned. Three such coincidences arose, none of them targets of
the fitting process. The clearest of these is that the per-second growth rate of TINT in
holdover equals the device's independently reported FEE, and that the
CSAC temperature tracks the PCB temperature at a constant offset
matching all four recorded pairs to within $0.07\,^\circ$C---neither
relation was fitted; both emerge from the model's structure. A third,
weaker, observation is that a multi-hour holdover yields the health
status \texttt{0x14}, the manual's own worked example
(\texttt{0x10}\,$\vert$\,\texttt{0x4}); we note that this follows in
part from implementing the documented bit rules and is therefore less
independent than the first two. We present these as corroborating
evidence, not proof; but a model built by fitting other quantities has
no reason to reproduce the first two unless its underlying structure is
right.

\subsection{Documented manual-versus-device discrepancies}
Building from recordings, rather than from the manual alone, exposed
several points where the documentation is insufficient or misleading
(Table~\ref{tab:discrepancies}). Each is a case in which a
documentation-only simulator would be wrong, and each is therefore
evidence that faithful behaviour must be aligned to observation.

Section~\ref{sec:validation}: a measurement stage runs the model,

\emph{Holdover.} For the device's reported FEE
($y_0\!\approx\!1.8\times10^{-11}$), the accumulated time error of
\eqref{eq:holdover} reaches roughly $65$~ns after one hour and crosses
the $210$~ns health threshold at about $3.25$~hours; after one day it is
near $1.5\,\mu$s, of which the quadratic ageing term contributes only a
few nanoseconds. The earlier static behaviour returned a constant
$850$~ns throughout holdover; the
difference between that constant and \eqref{eq:holdover} is the error
that a monitoring client would previously have been given no way to
detect.

\emph{Health.} During holdover the health register
(Fig.~\ref{fig:health}) sets \texttt{0x10} once the holdover exceeds
$60$~s and adds \texttt{0x4} once the phase offset passes $210$~ns,
yielding the combined value \texttt{0x14}---the manual's own worked
example---at about $3.25$~hours. Independently, in the first $200$~s
after power-on the register carries \texttt{0x8} (``run-time
$<200$~s''), clearing to \texttt{0x0} thereafter.

\emph{Warm-up.} From an assumed $25\,^\circ$C ambient, the modelled PCB
temperature reaches roughly $46\,^\circ$C within two minutes and settles
near $52.8\,^\circ$C within about five minutes, while GNSS lock (servo
state $6$, and hence \texttt{SYNC:LOCKED?}$=1$) is not reported until
about twenty minutes (Fig.~\ref{fig:warmup}). The two timescales are
distinct, and a client polling only \texttt{SYNC:LOCKED?} cannot
distinguish the intervening ``locking'' state from the initial warm-up.

\emph{Fidelity checks.} The CSAC temperature equals the PCB temperature
plus $1.32\,^\circ$C, reproducing all four recorded device pairs to
within $0.07\,^\circ$C (Fig.~\ref{fig:csac}); the EFControl absolute
value equals the relative value scaled by $200$, matching every recorded
pair exactly; the locked-loop TINT jitters within $0 \pm 12$~ns with no
net trend, whereas the holdover TINT of the same query accumulates past
$200$~ns over several hours, confirming that the two internal loops are
modelled as physically distinct. Repeated independent runs of any
measurement reproduce identical series (maximum difference $0$),
confirming determinism.

\section{Limitations}
The artefact is a digital model, not a shadow or a twin: it has no live
link to any physical unit, and does not update as the device ages. As
stated in Section~\ref{sec:models}, the measurement noise is
deterministic by design and does not reproduce the device's spectral,
Allan-deviation signature; consequently the simulator must not be used
to evaluate oscillator stability, only to test client software against
the device's interface and time-domain behaviour. Some recorded values
were untimestamped, so certain behaviours are validated as ranges and
relationships rather than as time-anchored points. Finally, validation
rests on a single device instance and a limited set of recordings;
broader hardware comparison remains future work.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
We have built a physically grounded, behaviourally validated simulator
of a third-party GNSS-disciplined rubidium oscillator from only its
vendor manual and a small set of protocol recordings, and we have set
out a method for arguing fidelity when access to the device is limited:
trace every parameter, test physical invariants automatically, seek the
reproduction of untuned behaviour, and document where the manual and the
device disagree. A natural next step, and the one that would change the
artefact's taxonomic status, is to let the simulator ingest periodic
device logs and self-calibrate, establishing the automated one-way link
